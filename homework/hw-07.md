# Part 1 

I found eight problems in the draft, and any one of them could fail a PCI audit on its own.

**1. Section 3** Vulnerabilities get found through "word of mouth" and "industry news," which isn't a process. There's no authoritative source, no way of ranking risk, and no software inventory to check advisories against. I'd replace it with: "The SOC Analyst reviews CISA KEV, NVD, and vendor advisories every business day, and the GRC Analyst ranks each applicable vulnerability within two business days."

**2. Section 4** Everything waits for one monthly window, and failed patches "carry forward" forever. A critical patch released the day after the window could sit for about a month, and there's no testing, no back-out plan, and no emergency path. I'd write: "Deadlines run from vendor release: Critical 7 days (72 hours if on KEV), High 14, Medium 30, Low 90. The CAB approves out-of-window changes, and every patch has a back-out plan."

**3. Section 5** Internal scans only happen once a year and only cover servers. There's no authenticated scanning, no external ASV scan at all, and the tool is whatever someone "chooses." My fix: "Authenticated internal scans run with Nessus Professional at defined frequencies, and external ASV scans run at least quarterly and after any significant change."

**4. Section 5** Findings get looked at "as time permits," so nobody owns them, nothing has a deadline, and nothing gets rescanned. I'd say: "Every confirmed vulnerability gets a ticket with an owner and a due date, and it's only closed after a Security Team rescan."

**5. Sections 5-6** This one is probably the worst. The same sysadmins find the problem, fix it, and check their own fix, and the CISO and Security Team aren't mentioned anywhere. The owner is just "IT Department." My fix: "The CISO owns this policy. The Security Team verifies, and System Administration and Network Engineering remediate. No one both remediates and verifies."

**6. Sections 7-8** Reporting happens "as needed," and the review schedule is whatever the IT Director feels like, with no minimum. There are no metrics and no evidence retention either. I'd write: "KPIs are reported monthly, evidence is kept seven years, and the policy is reviewed at least every 12 months."

# Part 2 

**Frameworks:** PCI DSS v4.0.1 and SOX ITGC. **Owner:** CISO. **Review:** at least every 12 months.

**My assumptions.** The Environment Profile doesn't cover everything, so I made a few assumptions. A "System Owner" is the business owner recorded for each system in the inventory, like the Controller for the financial-reporting databases. WSUS runs on a Windows Server 2022 host that gets recorded in the inventory within 14 days. Nessus Professional is a single-operator tool with no role-based access, so I designed around that. PostgreSQL 15 comes from the PostgreSQL project's own repository, since Ubuntu 22.04 ships version 14. Acme can't staff 24/7 monitoring, so after-hours is escalation only through a best-effort Security on-call rotation that the CISO confirms.

## 1. Policy Statement and Governance

I put this policy together to make sure we've got a solid, ironclad process for hunting down, ranking, fixing, and double-checking vulnerabilities across everything at Acme. If a machine touches cardholder data, handles login access, or feeds into our financial reporting, it falls right under this umbrella. That means we're covering 6 DMZ web servers, 10 app and API servers, 4 PostgreSQL databases, 2 domain controllers, 3 file/print servers, roughly 200 Windows 11 workstations, 14 network devices, and the patch systems themselves—Ansible, WSUS, and Nessus. The CISO owns the policy, with sign-offs from the CISO, IT Director, and CFO. Honestly, I built the whole thing to knock out the three big headaches our QSA called us out on in the last Report on Compliance: we had no risk-ranking process, no ASV contract, and no wall between the folks applying patches and the ones verifying them.

We're taking enforcement seriously here. If anyone blows past a Critical or High deadline without getting an approved exception first, it lands on the CISO's desk the next business day. From there, the CISO can order emergency change fixes or straight-up yank the asset off the Cardholder Data Environment to protect us. If someone tries to cheat the system—like closing a ticket without actually fixing anything—that goes straight to HR for disciplinary action. On top of that, letting a Critical or High issue sit around means instant PCI failure, and if it's sitting on one of our four database servers, it triggers a SOX control deficiency too.

To keep things running smoothly and keep the auditors happy, everyone has a specific lane. The CISO owns and approves the policy, handles Critical/High exceptions, signs the ASV contract, approves monthly KPIs, and updates the CFO every quarter. Our Security Engineer is the only person allowed to run Nessus, confirm findings within two business days, run verification rescans, and officially close out tickets. Our GRC Analyst takes care of risk ranking within two business days, runs the exception log, tracks SOX evidence and KPIs, approves false positives, and reviews scan scope every month. Meanwhile, our SOC Analyst keeps an eye on threat intel daily, opens tickets, handles after-hours emergency escalations, and serves as our backup scanner operator.

On the execution side, our four System Administrators fix server and workstation issues within SLA using Ansible for Linux and WSUS for Windows. They set up scan accounts, submit change requests with rollback plans, and mark tickets as remediated pending verification—though they can't close tickets or run verification scans themselves. Our two Network Engineers patch the 14 network devices under the same SLAs, keep our vendor list current, set up read-only scan access, and lock down assets in an emergency if the CISO calls for it. System Owners step in to approve downtime for High and Critical fixes, co-sign exceptions, and confirm system criticality once a year. Lastly, the Change Advisory Board—chaired by the IT Director with Security in the room—approves changes against our matrix, maintains pre-approved patch templates, and reviews any emergency or out-of-window requests.

## 2. CVSS Classification and SLA Matrix

I designed our vulnerability patching around a dual-clock system so nothing gets missed. The vendor-disclosure clock starts the second a vendor releases a fix. This measures our real exposure window and keeps us compliant with PCI 6.3.3's one-month rule. The scan-discovery clock starts when our periodic scans actually catch the issue. Whichever deadline hits first wins. If a scan finds an issue that is already past its vendor deadline, it gets flagged as overdue on day one.

To keep things honest, I start with NVD CVSS base scores and strictly enforce risk bumps. Anything on CISA KEV or actively exploited gets bumped to Critical. An EPSS probability of 0.50 or higher forces a rank of at least High. Internet exposure moves 7.0+ scores up a tier. Finally, core assets like CDE databases, domain controllers, and WSUS never drop below High. No one can downgrade a score on gut feel. The only way down is a documented "Not Applicable" finding approved by our GRC Analyst.

My proposed SLA tiers dictate both the remediation timeline and who signs off on the fix. For Critical flaws (CVSS 9.0–10.0 or KEV), I set a 7-day vendor SLA (72 hours for KEV) and a 5-day scan SLA (3 days for KEV). These run as emergency changes approved by the IT Director and Security within four business hours, with same-day CISO notification. High issues (CVSS 7.0–8.9) get 14 days from vendor disclosure and 10 from scan discovery, requiring an expedited CAB sign-off. Medium issues (CVSS 4.0–6.9) get 30 vendor days and 21 scan days as pre-approved standard changes with 3 days' notice to the owner. Low issues (CVSS 0.1–3.9) get 90 vendor days and 60 scan days within our normal quarterly cycle.

Here is how this framework applies to real CVEs from the last couple of years:
CVE-2025-59287 (WSUS RCE, CVSS 9.8): Classified as Critical on the 72-hour KEV track. Microsoft released the patch on Oct 23, 2025, and CISA listed it on Oct 24, setting the deadline to Oct 27. Because a compromised WSUS server threatens all Windows endpoints and domain controllers, it requires rapid emergency approval; CVE-2025-1094 (PostgreSQL SQLi, CVSS 8.1): Hits High on our four CDE database servers. Published Feb 13, 2025, its Feb 27 vendor deadline easily absorbs our Feb 22 maintenance window. It moves forward via an expedited CAB with the Controller signing off on the restart; CVE-2025-23419 (Nginx Client-Cert Bypass, CVSS 4.3): Classified as Medium with a Mar 7, 2025 standard-change deadline. If a Security Engineer verifies our DMZ servers do not use that specific client-cert setup, I can mark it Not Applicable and close it out with the attached config proof.

## 3. Identification and Scanning Program

I designed our operational framework around daily intelligence gathering, asset-tiered scanning, secure credentials, strict segregation of duties, and a clear verification lifecycle. To catch new threats early, my SOC Analyst reviews reputable threat sources every business day—including CISA KEV, NVD, CISA/US-CERT advisories, Microsoft MSRC, Ubuntu Security Notices, PostgreSQL and Nginx announcements, vendor PSIRT feeds, and FIRST EPSS scores—while KEV and NVD also trigger automated Jira alerts. Because our security team has only three members without 24/7 coverage, after-hours focus strictly on high-priority escalation: an automated KEV alert pages the on-call Security Engineer, who must acknowledge within two hours and request an emergency change while Network Engineering applies immediate containment.

Our scan schedule stays well ahead of PCI-DSS Requirement 11.3.2's quarterly minimum, and we file formal quarterly packages as evidence. DMZ servers and public interfaces get weekly credentialed scans, monthly external uncredentialed scans, and monthly-to-quarterly ASV scans. Core CDE systems, domain controllers, and WSUS patch servers get weekly credentialed scans, while internal workstations, file servers, and network devices are scanned monthly. Any asset modified during a major change is rescanned within five business days. To resolve our ASV compliance gap—since Nessus Professional cannot produce PCI-valid external reports—our CISO will contract an official ASV within 30 days and run the first scan within 60, using weekly external Nessus scans only as a temporary stopgap capped at 90 days.

We use Nessus Professional for both credentialed and uncredentialed scans, defaulting to credentialed because Linux package backporting often causes uncredentialed banner checks to report false positives on patched software. Sysadmins set up dedicated, restricted scan accounts (restricted sudoers on Linux, non-interactive GPO accounts on Windows) that are locked inside Nessus, ensuring the Security Team never holds production admin credentials and sysadmins cannot run scans. To satisfy PCI 11.3.1's segregation of duties, the person who fixes a flaw can never verify it, and sysadmin self-checks do not count as evidence. Only the Security Team can move Jira tickets to "Verified/Closed" based on fresh rescan data, while our GRC Analyst conducts monthly reviews of scan scope and credentials to keep management separate from reporting.

Finally, we enforce a strict workflow to turn raw scanner output into verified resolution. A raw scan output is just a "finding" with no SLA attached until a Security Engineer validates it within two business days by checking asset inventory, package versions, and active configurations to make it a "confirmed vulnerability." If a finding is a false positive, only the GRC Analyst can approve the exception, and all suppressions are re-evaluated every quarter. Once a fix is applied, the Security Engineer rescans the asset within two business days; if it fails, the ticket reopens with its original SLA clock still running, and we rescan iteratively until a clean result or approved GRC exception is achieved.

## 4. Exception and Risk Acceptance

If we can’t fix something by its deadline, I allow an exception, but only with the right people signing off and a hard time limit. For Critical issues, the CISO and System Owner approve (with the IT Director consulted), and it lasts 30 days, with one 30-day renewal that the CISO and CFO approve. High exceptions last up to 60 days with a 30-day renewal, same approvers. Medium and Low exceptions are lighter: the GRC Analyst and System Owner approve them (plus the CISO if it’s a CDE or financial database), and they run up to 90 or 180 days, each with one renewal of the same length. Nobody approves an exception on a system they administer. If a Critical or High exception reaches 90 days total, it has to become a funded remediation project approved by the CISO and CFO, because the QSA doesn’t like compensating controls that quietly become permanent.

For Critical and High exceptions, compensating controls have to cover the same attack path, following PCI Appendices B and C. That might mean network isolation, restricting ports, disabling the vulnerable feature, using the vendor workaround, daily SOC log review, tighter admin access, or weekly credentialed scans. Each request has to explain how the controls reduce the risk and how we’ll prove they’re working.

Every exception is a Jira Service Management ticket linked to the vulnerability, with the CVE, asset, rating, reason, controls, approvers, and expiry all required. The GRC Analyst exports the register monthly with a hash as seven-year SOX evidence. Reminders go out 30, 14, and 7 days before expiry, and the Security Engineer checks monthly that the controls are actually running. If an exception expires, the item goes back to overdue and escalates automatically.

I’d tell the CISO about any missed Critical or High deadline without an exception, any KEV match on an Acme asset, and any exception request involving a database server, CDE system, domain controller, or patch infrastructure. I’d also escalate Critical or High renewals, failed compensating controls, disputed ratings or refused downtime, two failed rescans, ASV failures or missed quarters, assets missing from the inventory, CDE scan gaps, and any ticket closed without a Security rescan.

## 5. KPIs (monthly to the CISO and IT Director, quarterly to the CFO)

I report these monthly to the CISO and IT Director, and quarterly to the CFO.

For SLA compliance, I divide the number fixed by the deadline by the number due that month. My targets are 100% for Critical, 95% or better for High, 90% for Medium, and 85% for Low. This maps to PCI 6.3.3 and 11.3.1 and to SOX change and operations controls.

For mean time to remediate, I average the days between the vendor’s release date and our fix date, by tier. I’m aiming for 5 days or less for Critical (72 hours for KEV), 10 days for High, and 25 days for Medium. This maps to PCI 6.3.3.

For credentialed scan coverage, I divide assets scanned with credentials by total assets in the inventory. The target is 100% for the CDE and domain controllers and 98% or better overall. This maps to PCI 11.3.1, 11.3.1.2, and 12.5.1 and to SOX scope completeness.

For independent verification rate, I divide closed tickets verified by a Security rescan by all closed tickets. The target is 100%, tied to PCI 11.3.1 and SOX separation of duties.

For exception health, I count open Critical and High exceptions and any past expiry. I want no more than two open at once and none past expiry. This maps to PCI Appendices B and C and 12.3.1 and to SOX risk-acceptance evidence.

For KEV response, I track how many matching entries we triage within one business day and how many we fix or mitigate within 72 hours. The target is 100% for both, tied to PCI 6.3.1 and 6.3.3.

For external scan compliance, I count how many of the last four quarters had a passing ASV report. The target is 4 of 4, tied to PCI 11.3.2.

All of this goes into a write-protected evidence repository: reports, scan exports, Jira histories, and exception exports. I keep it seven years for SOX-relevant systems and at least 12 months for everything else, so an auditor can follow any database vulnerability from first alert through ranking, CAB approval, the fix, the rescan, and closure.
