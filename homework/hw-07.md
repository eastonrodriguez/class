# Part 1 

I found eight problems in the draft, and any one of them could fail a PCI audit on its own.

**1. Header (12.1.1, 12.1.2).** The first thing I noticed is that the policy is written for PCI DSS v3.2.1, which was retired in March 2024, and it's dated 2022 with no sign anyone has reviewed it since. I'd fix it with something like: "This policy implements PCI DSS v4.0.1 and SOX ITGC. The CISO reviews it at least every 12 months and after any significant change."

**2. Section 2, Scope (6.3.3, 12.5.1, 12.5.2).** The draft leaves out network devices and just says to see a runbook that doesn't exist. That's a problem because Acme has 14 network devices and some sit at the CDE boundary, and 6.3.3 covers all system components. My fix: "This policy covers every component in or connected to the CDE and every SOX financial-reporting system, including network devices and the patch infrastructure. Scope is confirmed at least every 12 months."

**3. Section 3, Identification (6.3.1, 6.3.2).** Vulnerabilities get found through "word of mouth" and "industry news," which isn't a process. There's no authoritative source, no way of ranking risk, and no software inventory to check advisories against. I'd replace it with: "The SOC Analyst reviews CISA KEV, NVD, and vendor advisories every business day, and the GRC Analyst ranks each applicable vulnerability within two business days."

**4. Section 4, Timelines (6.3.3, 6.5.1, 6.5.2).** Everything waits for one monthly window, and failed patches "carry forward" forever. A critical patch released the day after the window could sit for about a month, and there's no testing, no back-out plan, and no emergency path. I'd write: "Deadlines run from vendor release: Critical 7 days (72 hours if on KEV), High 14, Medium 30, Low 90. The CAB approves out-of-window changes, and every patch has a back-out plan."

**5. Section 5, Scanning (11.3.1, 11.3.1.2, 11.3.1.3, 11.3.2, 11.3.2.1).** Internal scans only happen once a year and only cover servers. There's no authenticated scanning, no external ASV scan at all, and the tool is whatever someone "chooses." My fix: "Authenticated internal scans run with Nessus Professional at defined frequencies, and external ASV scans run at least quarterly and after any significant change."

**6. Section 5, Findings (11.3.1, 6.3.3).** Findings get looked at "as time permits," so nobody owns them, nothing has a deadline, and nothing gets rescanned. I'd say: "Every confirmed vulnerability gets a ticket with an owner and a due date, and it's only closed after a Security Team rescan."

**7. Sections 5-6, Roles (11.3.1, 6.1.2, 12.1.3, 12.1.4).** This one is probably the worst. The same sysadmins find the problem, fix it, and check their own fix, and the CISO and Security Team aren't mentioned anywhere. The owner is just "IT Department." My fix: "The CISO owns this policy. The Security Team verifies, and System Administration and Network Engineering remediate. No one both remediates and verifies."

**8. Sections 7-8, Reporting and Review (12.1.2, and 12.4.1-12.4.2 if Acme counts as a service provider).** Reporting happens "as needed," and the review schedule is whatever the IT Director feels like, with no minimum. There are no metrics and no evidence retention either. I'd write: "KPIs are reported monthly, evidence is kept seven years, and the policy is reviewed at least every 12 months."

I also noticed the draft is missing a few things entirely: an exception and risk-acceptance process (12.3.1, Appendices B and C), an emergency patching path (6.3.3, 6.5.1), and any handling for end-of-life software.

# Part 2 

**Frameworks:** PCI DSS v4.0.1 and SOX ITGC. **Owner:** CISO. **Review:** at least every 12 months.

**My assumptions.** The Environment Profile doesn't cover everything, so I made a few assumptions. (A1) A "System Owner" is the business owner recorded for each system in the inventory, like the Controller for the financial-reporting databases. (A2) WSUS runs on a Windows Server 2022 host that gets recorded in the inventory within 14 days. (A3) Nessus Professional is a single-operator tool with no role-based access, so I designed around that. (A4) PostgreSQL 15 comes from the PostgreSQL project's own repository, since Ubuntu 22.04 ships version 14. (A5) Acme can't staff 24/7 monitoring, so after-hours is escalation only through a best-effort Security on-call rotation that the CISO confirms.

## 1. Policy Statement and Governance

**Purpose and authority.** The goal is simple: Acme finds, ranks, fixes, and independently verifies vulnerabilities on everything that touches cardholder data, authenticates access to it, or supports financial reporting. That's 6 DMZ web servers, 10 app/API servers, 4 PostgreSQL servers, 2 domain controllers, 3 file/print servers, about 200 Windows 11 workstations, 14 network devices, and the patch infrastructure itself (Ansible, WSUS, Nessus). The CISO owns the policy, and the CISO, IT Director, and CFO approve it. I built it to fix the three problems the QSA flagged in the last Report on Compliance: no risk-ranking process (6.3.1), no ASV contract (11.3.2), and no separation between the people patching and the people verifying (11.3.1).

**What happens if people don't follow it.** If a Critical or High deadline is missed without an approved exception, it goes to the CISO the next business day. The CISO can require emergency change handling or even isolate the asset from the CDE. Deliberate violations, like closing a ticket without actually fixing anything, go through Acme's disciplinary process. And an unfixed Critical or High finding is a PCI failure, and for the four database servers it's also a SOX control deficiency.

**Who does what.** The **CISO** owns and approves the policy, grants Critical and High exceptions, signs the ASV contract, signs off on the monthly KPI package, and reports to the CFO each quarter. The **Security Engineer** is the only person who runs Nessus. They run the scans, confirm findings within two business days, do every verification rescan, and are the only role that can close a ticket. The **GRC Analyst** ranks risk within two business days, runs the exception register, handles SOX evidence and the KPIs, approves false positives, and reviews scan scope every month. The **SOC Analyst** checks the intelligence sources each business day, opens tickets, handles after-hours KEV escalation, and is the trained backup scan operator. The four **System Administrators** fix things within the SLA using Ansible for Linux and WSUS for Windows, file changes with rollback plans, and mark tickets "Remediated - Pending Verification." They can't close tickets or run verification scans, and they set up the scan accounts. The two **Network Engineers** patch the 14 devices on the same SLAs, give me the vendor list, set up read-only scan access, and apply emergency containment if the CISO asks. **System Owners** approve downtime for High and above, co-sign exceptions, and confirm each system's criticality once a year. The **CAB** (IT Director chairs, with a Security rep) approves changes per the matrix, keeps pre-approved templates for routine patching, and signs off on out-of-window or emergency changes.

## 2. CVSS Classification and SLA Matrix

**Why there are two clocks.** The vendor-disclosure clock starts when the vendor ships a complete fix, and it measures how long Acme is exposed to a publicly known flaw, which is what PCI 6.3.3's "within one month of release" is about. The scan-discovery clock starts when a scan finding is confirmed, and it measures how slow we are at noticing. I need both because Acme only scans weekly to monthly, so a flaw could sit there for weeks before a scan sees it. Whichever deadline comes first wins, and if a scan finds something already past its vendor deadline, it's overdue the day it's confirmed.

**How I rank risk (6.3.1).** I start with the NVD CVSS base score, or the vendor's score if NVD hasn't analyzed it yet. Anything on CISA KEV or being actively exploited becomes Critical, and anything with an EPSS probability of 0.50 or higher becomes at least High. If the asset is internet-exposed and the score is 7.0 or higher, it moves up one tier. CDE systems, financial databases, domain controllers, and the patch infrastructure never rank below High at 7.0 or above. I don't allow downgrades based on gut feel. The only way down is a documented "Not Applicable" finding that the GRC Analyst approves.

**The SLA tiers.**
- **Critical (CVSS 9.0-10.0, or anything on KEV or actively exploited):** 7 days from vendor disclosure, or 72 hours for KEV, and 5 days from scan discovery (3 for KEV). The fix goes through as an emergency change approved by the IT Director and a Security rep within four business hours, and the CISO and System Owner are told the same day.
- **High (7.0-8.9):** 14 days from vendor disclosure and 10 from scan discovery. An expedited CAB approves it, with the Security rep and System Owner signing off, and the IT Director has to approve anything out-of-window.
- **Medium (4.0-6.9):** 30 days from vendor disclosure and 21 from scan discovery. It's a pre-approved standard change, and I'd tell the System Owner three business days ahead.
- **Low (0.1-3.9):** 90 days from vendor disclosure and 60 from scan discovery, as a pre-approved standard change in the quarterly cycle.

**Real CVEs I classified** (scores are what the NVD-fed records showed):

**CVE-2025-59287 (WSUS remote code execution, CVSS 9.8)** is Critical on the 72-hour KEV track. Microsoft's complete out-of-band fix came out Oct 23, 2025 and CISA listed it on Oct 24, so I'd set the deadline at Oct 27, 2025. This one matters a lot for Acme because everything on Windows gets patched through WSUS, so a compromised WSUS server reaches every Windows machine, including the domain controllers.

**CVE-2025-1094 (PostgreSQL libpq SQL injection, fixed in 15.11, CVSS 8.1)** is High. It hits Acme's four CDE database servers. It was published Feb 13, 2025, so the deadline is Feb 27, and the Feb 22 maintenance window lands inside that. I'd run it through an expedited CAB, with the Controller approving the database restart.

**CVE-2025-23419 (nginx TLS session-resumption client-certificate bypass, CVSS 4.3)** is Medium. It only matters on the DMZ Nginx servers if they use client-certificate authentication on a shared default server. The deadline is Mar 7, 2025 as a standard change, and if the Security Engineer checks the config and that setup isn't there, I'd mark it Not Applicable and attach the evidence.

## 3. Identification and Scanning Program

**Where I get vulnerability info.** The SOC Analyst checks these every business day: CISA KEV, NVD, Ubuntu Security Notices, Microsoft MSRC and Patch Tuesday, PostgreSQL and nginx security announcements, network-vendor PSIRT feeds, CISA/US-CERT advisories, and FIRST EPSS. KEV and NVD also trigger automatic Jira alerts. The Security Team is only three people with no 24/7 coverage, so after hours is about escalation, not monitoring. If a KEV match comes in, the Security on-call gets paged, acknowledges within two hours, and requests an emergency change, and Network Engineering can apply containment while the patch is being staged.

**How often I scan.** Everything gets scanned more often than PCI's three-month minimum for internal scans, and I'd file a quarterly scan package as evidence. The six DMZ servers and externally reachable device interfaces get weekly credentialed scans plus monthly uncredentialed scans from an attacker's point of view. They also get an ASV scan at least quarterly (I'm aiming for monthly) and after any significant change. The CDE app and database servers, CDE-boundary devices, domain controllers, and patch infrastructure get weekly credentialed scans. The three file/print servers and the other network devices get scanned monthly. Workstations get a monthly credentialed scan plus a verification check ten days after each Patch Tuesday. And every asset gets scanned within five business days of a significant change.

**The ASV gap (RoC 11.3.2).** Nessus Professional can't produce a PCI-valid external scan, so Acme needs a real ASV. I'd have the CISO sign a contract within 30 days and get the first scan done within 60. Until then, the Security Engineer runs weekly external Nessus scans as a temporary stopgap. It isn't PCI-valid, it ends when the contract starts, and it can never go past 90 days. A passing ASV report means nothing at CVSS 4.0 or higher.

**Tools and credentials.** I'm using Nessus Professional for both credentialed and uncredentialed scans, and I default to credentialed because Ubuntu backports security fixes and the version banner can look old even when the fix is installed. The sysadmins set up the scan accounts (restricted sudoers on Linux, a GPO-scoped non-interactive account on Windows) and the accounts only get used through Nessus. That way the Security Team never holds production admin credentials, and the sysadmins can't use Nessus.

**Separation of duties (RoC 11.3.1).** My rule is simple: whoever fixes it never verifies it. Sysadmin self-checks don't count as evidence, and sysadmins aren't allowed to run Nessus. In Jira, only the Security Team can move a ticket to "Verified/Closed," and they need rescan evidence to do it. The GRC Analyst also reviews scan scope and credentials every month so no single person controls what gets scanned and what gets reported.

**Findings, false positives, and rescanning.** A *finding* is just raw scanner output and has no SLA. A *confirmed vulnerability* is a finding the Security Engineer has checked within two business days. They confirm the asset is in the inventory, that credentialed package or version evidence backs it up, and that the vulnerable configuration is actually present. Only the GRC Analyst can approve a false positive, and I'd re-review every suppression each quarter. After a fix, the Security Engineer rescans within two business days. If it fails, the ticket reopens with the original clock still running, and we keep rescanning until it clears or an approved exception exists.

## 4. Exception and Risk Acceptance

**Who can grant an exception and for how long.** A **Critical** exception needs the CISO and System Owner, with the IT Director consulted, and it lasts at most 30 days, with one renewal of up to 30 days that needs the CISO and CFO (60 total). A **High** exception needs the CISO and System Owner and lasts at most 60 days, with one renewal of up to 30 days that needs the CISO and CFO (90 total). A **Medium** exception needs the GRC Analyst and System Owner (plus the CISO if it's a CDE or financial database) and lasts at most 90 days with one 90-day renewal. A **Low** exception needs the GRC Analyst and System Owner and lasts at most 180 days with one 180-day renewal. Nobody can approve an exception on a system they administer. If a Critical or High exception hits 90 days total, it has to turn into a funded remediation project that the CISO and CFO approve. I did this because the QSA doesn't like compensating controls that quietly become permanent.

**Compensating controls.** For Critical and High exceptions, the controls have to cover the same attack path, following PCI Appendices B and C. Examples are network isolation or port restrictions (like limiting WSUS ports), turning off the vulnerable feature or using the vendor workaround, daily SOC log review for signs of exploitation, locking down admin access, and keeping up weekly credentialed scans. The request has to explain how each control lowers the risk and how we'll prove it's working.

**How I track and review them.** Every exception is a Jira Service Management ticket linked to the vulnerability, and CVE, asset, rating, reason, controls, approvers, and expiry are all required fields. The GRC Analyst exports the register monthly with a hash as seven-year SOX evidence. Reminders fire at 30, 14, and 7 days before expiry, and the Security Engineer checks monthly that the controls are really running. If an exception expires, the item goes back to overdue and escalates automatically.

**When the CISO has to be told.** I'd escalate any Critical or High deadline that's missed without an exception, any KEV match on an Acme asset, and any exception request involving a database server, CDE system, domain controller, or patch infrastructure. Any Critical or High renewal goes up too, along with a failed compensating control, a disputed rating or refused downtime, two failed rescans, an ASV failure or missed quarter, an asset that isn't in the inventory or a CDE scan gap, and any ticket closed without a Security rescan.

## 5. KPIs (monthly to the CISO and IT Director, quarterly to the CFO)

**SLA compliance by tier** is the number fixed by the governing deadline divided by the number due that month, times 100. I'm targeting 100% for Critical, at least 95% for High, 90% for Medium, and 85% for Low. It maps to PCI 6.3.3 and 11.3.1 and to SOX change and operations controls.

**Mean time to remediate** is the sum of (fix date minus vendor release date) divided by the number remediated, by tier. I'm targeting 5 days or less for Critical (72 hours for KEV), 10 days for High, and 25 days for Medium. It maps to PCI 6.3.3.

**Credentialed scan coverage** is assets scanned with credentials divided by assets in the inventory, times 100. The target is 100% for the CDE and domain controllers and at least 98% overall. It maps to PCI 11.3.1, 11.3.1.2, and 12.5.1 and to SOX scope completeness.

**Independent verification rate** is closed tickets verified by a Security rescan divided by all closed tickets, times 100. The target is 100%. It maps to PCI 11.3.1 and to SOX separation of duties.

**Exception health** counts open Critical and High exceptions and exceptions past expiry. I want no more than two open at once and zero past expiry. It maps to PCI Appendices B and C and 12.3.1 and to SOX risk-acceptance evidence.

**KEV response** is the share of matching KEV entries triaged within one business day, plus the share fixed or mitigated within 72 hours. The target is 100% for both, and it maps to PCI 6.3.1 and 6.3.3.

**External scan compliance** is how many of the last four quarters had a passing ASV report. The target is 4 of 4, and it maps to PCI 11.3.2.

Everything goes into a write-protected evidence repository: reports, scan exports, Jira histories, and exception exports. I'd keep them seven years for SOX-relevant systems and at least 12 months for the rest, so an auditor can follow any database vulnerability from the first alert through ranking, CAB approval, the fix, the rescan, and closure.
